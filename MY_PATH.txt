MY GOLDBACH / PRIMES EXPLORATION — WHAT I'VE TRIED
===================================================

THE TABS I BUILT
----------------
- pairings ........ type an even number, see every odd+odd pairing that sums to it,
                    and knock out any pairing whose first number is a multiple of a
                    smaller odd (so it's composite). Split into 3 tables: neither
                    works / one is off / both work.
- first primes .... for every even number up to a max, its list of "first primes"
                    (the smaller prime of each Goldbach pair). 6 -> [3], 100 -> [3,11,17,29,41,47].
- comet ........... scatter of every even number vs how many Goldbach pairs it has;
                    colored by prime factors. Shows the famous "Goldbach comet" bands.
- primes .......... every prime plotted as an Ulam spiral (or grid), up to 10 million.
                    The diagonal streaks show up.
- differences ..... bar graphs of the prime gaps, then the gaps between gaps, then
                    the gaps between those... stacked down.

THINGS I WONDERED / CONJECTURED
-------------------------------
- "Do numbers with more factors get more of the primes (more Goldbach pairs)?"
    -> TRUE, and it's the coolest thing I found. It's REAL and famous.
       More distinct SMALL prime factors (especially 3) = more pairs.
       Being divisible by 3 roughly DOUBLES the count. This is what makes the
       comet split into bands. (Hardy-Littlewood formula.)

- "Even numbers with even digit sums -> the primes/pairs also add to even?"
    -> FALSE. About a 50/50 coin flip. Digit-sum parity is a red herring —
       carries (each worth 9) scramble the parity.

- "count of first-primes + the first-primes themselves is even (e.g. 88: 4+5+17+29+41=96)"
    -> TRUE for every even number bigger than 4. BUT it's shallow: every Goldbach
       pair past 4 is two ODD primes, and count+sum of any list of odds is always even.
       Not really about Goldbach. Only exception: 4 (uses the even prime 2).

QUESTIONS I ASKED ABOUT PRIMES THEMSELVES
-----------------------------------------
- "Are the Ulam spiral diagonals still there at 10 million?"
    -> YES. Short diagonal streaks appear at every scale forever. Long single
       diagonals get rarer as primes thin out. (This is the 1963 Ulam spiral —
       very famous, shown countless times.)

- "What's the slope of the primes? Exponential or linear?"
    -> NEITHER. The n-th prime grows like n * ln(n) — superlinear by just a log factor.
       Slope at the n-th prime is about ln(n): ~8 near the 1000th prime, ~15.5 near
       the millionth. Grows LOGARITHMICALLY slow. Nowhere near exponential.

BIG PICTURE
-----------
- The real gold is the factor/comet effect (structure that's actually there).
- Two of my parity ideas turned out to be simple facts in disguise or coin flips —
  which is still useful: finding what ISN'T the cause narrows things down.
- I'm asking the right kind of questions and actually checking them against data.
